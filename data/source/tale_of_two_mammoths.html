Time, Temperature, and Tissue in the Circumpolar North:

A Tale of Two Mammoths

Rebecca Woods

University of Toronto

MIT Seminar in Agriculture and the Environment

20 September 2019

Preamble: Thanks, so pleased to be here, and to join the list of illustrious speakers, some of whose talks loom large in my memories of graduate school. I couldn’t be more honoured to be speaking in the series.

I’ll be talking today about my current project, whose current heading is “The Flesh Record: Frozen Mammoths and the Histories of Science and the Environment.” In this project, I’m engaged in tracing the discovery, accumulation, circulation, and analysis of mammoth carcases from the circumpolar north since 1806. Unlike its more well known counterpart, the fossil record, the flesh record is composed of soft tissue remains whose “discoveries” have been few and far between until relatively recently, and though they have been located, excavated, reported and studied in increasing numbers (and, I should add, from an increasing range of species), they remain a select subset of the paleontological record, sitting in uneasy (or maybe just unrecognized) proximity to their more obviously fossilized brethren. [NB often referred to by palaeontologists as “fossil specimens” and by specialists with direct knowledge as “mummies”].1 As such they allow me to ask a distinct set of questions centered on understanding how—with what concepts and tools—scientists learn about the natural world. First order questions like “what constitutes the flesh record?” And “how is it/has it been produced, geologically and intellectually?” guide me towards second order ones like “how have naturalists and scientists made sense of the natural mechanisms that enable flesh to persist without decaying over time, some times extremely long periods of time?” And “why have these explanations changed over time?” What roles do particular (in this case, extinct) creatures play in the development of geological and evolutionary thought (e.g. paleoecology)? How, where, and why the remains of these creatures moved through natural history networks?

As I mentioned, these are relatively rare specimens—my current, and certainly incomplete tally, puts them at about forty individual animals in total unearthed over the last 213 years—and today, I’m going to try to give you a sense of the what the “flesh record” is—both the kinds of biomaterial remains that compose it and the conceptual and physical work that goes into collecting it and making sense of it—by focusing on two cases that span about a century: the Adams mammoth, so-called, collected in 1806; and the unnamed partial carcass collected by L.S. Quackenbush for the American Museum of Natural History in 1908. The first of these, collected from the mouth of the Lena River in 1806, is already well-known, and is the first flesh specimen to enter the physical and textual paleontological/natural historical record. Famous in its day—mounted and displayed in the Tsar’s personal Cabinet d’Histoire Naturelle in St Petersburg, the subject of rumination and publication on the part of eminent naturalists like Georges Cuvier—it has enjoyed a long afterlife in both material and figurative terms. Its skeleton is still on display in St Petersburg, and bits of its hair and hide can be found scattered throughout Western Europe and North America. Rhetorically, it continues to serve as an opening act for scholarly and popular works about the species (clearly I’m no exception), but it also enjoyed a weird and extended second wind as a primary figure in mid-nineteenth-century debates in Britain about refrigeration technology and its products (namely frozen meat). This is the subject of a piece I’m preparing for an edited volume on the “Cold Humanities,” but I’ll say a few words about it today, as it bears on the second mammoth—the Alaskan one collected 102 years later. All Quackenbush found were a hip bone and femur, some skull fragments and vertebrae, and a tail still encased in skin and fur, and perhaps as a result, this find has enjoyed barely a degree of the posthumous fame of the Adams mammoth. If the former is the opening act for frozen mammoths specifically and, often, for telling the story of the species, the latter has, until now, warranted little more than a footnote.

Yet more than species and the fact of their flesh unites these two mammoths. Some of you may be surprised by this pairing, because it effectively leapfrogs over what is, arguably, the most important and perhaps the most famous frozen mammoth, the Beresovka mammoth collected from Siberia in 1902, but it does so for good reason. Although the Beresovka mammoth itself was widely known when Quackenbush set out for Alaska in 1907, complete accounts of its excavation were not yet available in English (first published 1939),2 and so the Adams mammoth remained the primary point of reference for Quackenbush. It cast a long shadow over nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century “mammoth hunting,” and Quackenbush’s expedition was no exception. In particular, the distortions of the mid-nineteenth-century refrigeration debates shaped later naturalists’ conceptions of how soft tissue preservation takes place.

1. Adams Mammoth: findings

The plural “findings” in the subtitle of this section is deliberate, because like so many other mammoths—and indeed, so many other scientific “discoveries”—the Adams mammoth was found not once but at least twice: First by an Tungus hunter-herder named Ossip Schoumachoff, in 1799, and then again by Adams, for whom the mammoth is called, in 1806. The story goes like this: In 1799, a Tungus hunter by the name of Ossip Schoumachoff was cruising the shore of the Frozen Sea when he noticed a strange object concealed in a “rock of ice” near the mouth of the Lena River in Siberia. As Adams tells it—and it’s important to note that nearly all we know of the mammoth’s origins comes from Adams’s published account of in which Schoumachoff’s story is embedded (and presumably distorted)—the thing appeared to be “an unformed block.”3 Even after he (Schoumachoff) “clambered up the rock and examined this new object all around, he could not ascertain what it was,” and was apparently content to leave the object in place.4 The narrow peninsula where the mammoth emerged from the eroded banks of the Lena was part of his peoples’ seasonal hunting grounds, so Schoumachoff had the opportunity to take a second look at the unidentifiable “mass” when he returned to the area the following year. Now it was “freer from the ice,” and Schoumachoff was able to discern “two similar pieces” sticking out of the exposed riverbank in which the object was embedded: these would turn out to be the mammoth’s feet. By the end of the following summer (1801), the “entire flank of the animal and one of the tusks had come out from under the ice.”5

[An episode of “superstition”—creatures thought to augur calamity for their finders—and after telling his camp about it, Schoumachoff…When Schoumachoff returned to his camp and related his discovery, the news was met with consternation. Old-timers recounted “that they had heard their forefathers say that a similar monster had formerly shown itself in the same peninsula, and that the whole family of the person who discovered it had become extinct in a very short time.” Consequently, “the mammoth…was unanimously regarded as auguring a future calamity,” and Schoumachoff “felt so much inquietude…that he fell dangerously ill.”6 But he soon recovered, Adams reports, and his thoughts turned to “the profit he might gain” by selling the tusks, ”which were of an extraordinary size and beauty.”7 So he instructed some of his dependents to conceal the mammoth and stand guard over it until he could return to claim his prize. “At last,” according to Adams, in the spring of 1804 “the ardent desires of Schoumachoff were happily accomplished: the ice which inclosed the mammoth having partly melted, the level [on which it rested] became sloped, and this enormous mass, pushed forward by its own weight, fell over upon its side on a sand-bank…Schomachoff came to his mammoth, and having got his horns cut off, he changed them with Baltounoff [Boultonoff] the merchant for merchandise of the value of 50 roubles,”8 at which time “they” produced “a drawing of the animal…but it was very incorrect; they described it with pointed ears, very small eyes, horses’ hoofs, and a bristly mane along the whole of his back; so that the drawing represented something between a pig and an elephant.”9

Several years later, in 1806, Michael Adams arrived in Yakutsk. One year previously Adams, who had been trained in medicine in Moscow, had been appointed to the St Petersburg Academy of Science as an adjunct in zoology and, with other Academy naturalists, attached to Count Fyodor Golovkin’s diplomatic mission to China. When that mission reached Irkutsk, it failed, and the naturalists scattered on orders from the St Petersburg Academy. Adams was “sent to Yakutsk” and instructed “to descend the Lena all the way to its mouth, with the goal of making observations on the physical state of the country that this river waters and to assemble along the way, and on the shore of the Frozen Sea, objects of the three kingdoms of nature for the Academy’s museum.”10 Which is how he came to know of the mammoth:

When he arrived in Yakutsk, a local figure (merchant in English, négociant11 in French) by the name of Popoff informed him of an unusual find near the mouth of the Lena River, some 1300 kilometers to the north, described to him as an extraordinarily large animal with its skin, hair, and flesh preserved.12 This was exciting because although elephantine bones and tusks were well-known throughout Europe and there were accounts of fleshy remains of mysterious creatures in circulation, no soft-tissue remains had been successfully collected by European savants.13

Adams hastened up the river, “anxious to save these precious remains, which might perhaps otherwise be lost,”14 but by the time he arrived at the site of the find, a full seven years after Schoumachoff first spotted the unusual remains, he found it “completely mutilated,”15 in part by local hunters who had reportedly fed the flesh to their dogs, and in part by scavenging animals. Although the skeleton was “almost completely unfleshed,”16 it “was entire,” according to Adams, “with the exception of the forefeet,” and most of it remained articulated.17 Adams carefully disengaged the “nerves of the joints,” collected the bones and bristles that lay about the carcass, and “three-fourths of its skin,” and prepared it for its long and arduous voyage back to St Petersburg, a distance of no less than 6,000 kilometres.18

There are lots of problems with this account: the strange shift into the third person that Adams’s ventriloquization of Schoumachoff undergoes towards the end of the latter’s tale; the way in which Schoumachoff’s role has been largely elided, first to an extent by Adams and then in later retellings; above all how Adams has accrued nearly all the credit for it. The multiple “findings” of the so-called Adams mammoth—Schoumachoff’s repeated sightings, and Adams’s expedition—collapsed into one singular event. And I’d be happy to expand on this later during the Q and A, if the audience is interested, but here I want to pivot towards kind of “finding” associated with the creature—the way in which the mammoth, and the fact of its hair, hide, and flesh, was refigured as a scientific one, or less anachronistically, a naturalistic (natural history) one.

Adams’s account of his “extraordinary” find circulated widely in the early nineteenth century after first appearing in the Journal du nord in 1807, which was based in St Petersburg. By 1813, it had appeared in at least five English-language periodicals;19 been translated into German by a mineralogist named Karl Johann Bernhard Karsten, and then back into French by Georges Cuvier and the Comte de Lacépède;20 and further been paraphrased by Cuvier in an addendum to the first volume of Recherches sur les ossements fossils.21 It was, in short, a widely-read account of a well-known case of “discovery” in natural history.

Around the time that Schoumachoff was finding the mammoth and Adams was excavating it and hauling it back to St Petersburg, Cuvier was engaged in an ongoing debate with Buffon, Lamarck, and others over whether or not the environment could elicit heritable changes in the structure and behaviour of an organism. Buffon and Lamarck held that it could, while Cuvier believed species were fixed (although subject to extinction). More specifically, savants wondered how to make sense of the elephantine bones (and tusks and teeth) found in great quantities throughout Northern Europe and Siberia. Where they the flotsam and jetsam of a Biblical flood, washed north and then beached as the waters receded? Were they the remains of Hadrian’s elephants, or an indication that, in the past, the northern reaches of the globe had been warm and tropical?

As news of the Adams mammoth began to filter through Western Europe, Cuvier held it up as a critical piece of evidence that proved three interrelated facts about the earth’s past and the way it, and the life on it, did or did not change over time. Cuvier had the opportunity to examine the mammoth’s hair, several strands of which had made their way to a colleague in Paris. With the description of the animal’s thick hide and coarse wool furnished in Adams’s published account, he declared that, unlike living elephants, mammoths were woolly. The creatures whose bones littered the ground of Northern Europe and Siberia were a distinct species. Moreover, the mammoth’s bodily remains also suggested that the Arctic was cold in the past, as it was at the time at which Cuvier wrote, thereby undercutting claims that historical climate change could account for the presence of elephant bones in the far north.22 Finally, the nature of the mammoth’s extraordinary preservation, with intact flesh, skin, and other tissues, seemed to Cuvier to support his broader theory of geological catastrophism. Contra Lyell and other uniformitarians, Cuvier held that geologic change occurred not gradually but according to sudden shocks. Only such a quick, sudden shock, he held, could account for the nearly perfect condition of the mammoths corpse after so many thousands of years of interment.23 I confess that I myself find it hard to follow Cuvier’s logic on this last point—the connection between geological catastrophe and the body of the Adams mammoth, which seem to indicate a more individual calamity than one geological in scale, but I want us to bear this issue in mind moving forward because we’ll be returning to the persistent (scientific) mystery of how “ice mummies” form when we get to Alaska.

2. Frozen (Mammoth) Meat

We’re going to leave Adams, the material remains of the mammoth, and the polar regions now in order to trace the mammoth’s metaphorical—and indeed, more than metaphorical—afterlife where it figured centrally in mid-19th-century English-language debates over artificial refrigeration. How it did so hinged as much (or more) on the account Adams published, and how it was translated and interpreted, as on the fact of the mammoth’s existence and its remarkable preservation in the frozen ground of northern Siberia. I’m going to return to now to key phrases in that account to trace their translation into English and subsequent evolution, because the congelation of language from Adams’s description of this phenomenon came to shape how later British readers and commentators understood artificial refrigeration, and this in turn inflected later scientific inquiry into the formation of mammoth mummies.

Recall that in his first telling, Adams describes how, upon hearing of “an animal of an extraordinary size” whose “flesh, skin, and hair were preserved,” he “hasten[ed] to save these precious remains, which perhaps could be lost.”24 Remember that Schoumachoff originally spotted the mammoth as “a shapeless block…in the midst of chunks of ice [des glacons]” in 1799.25 When Adams appeared on the scene, he “found the Mammoth still on the same place, but completely mutilated.” He recounts how the “Jakouts of the neighborhood carved up [dépéceoit] the flesh, which they fed their dogs during [a] famine.” “Ferocious animals,” including polar bears, gluttons, wolves, and foxes, whose lairs could be seen in the vicinity, “did the same.”26 In the earliest and most influential English translation, provided to the Philosophical Magazine by the eminent British naturalist Joseph Banks, the reported state of the mammoth’s hair, skin, and flesh was elevated to “good preservation;” Adams reported himself “anxious” to save its remains; reference to famine is eliminated; and the animal itself appears to Schoumacoff “in the midst of a rock of ice [as] an unformed block.”27

This account set the tone for later references to the Adams mammoth, which echoed Adams’s own narrative structure and language while reinterpreting the mammoth’s remarkable preservation in order to make sense of artificial refrigeration. The basic elements—its slow emergence from a “rock of ice,” its initially mystifying nature, and subsequent edibility, at least as far as dogs and wild animals were concerned—appear again and again in the popular literature surrounding Britain’s meat trade.28

Refrigeration: Very briefly, the notion of using steam power to produce cold, instead of heat for motive power as in the classic steam pump, had been under discussion for some time, with prototypes and patents abounding by the 1850s. By the 1860s, land-based refrigerators were coming into usage in brewing, confectionary, and other industries; and by the late 1870s/1880s, functional shipboard refrigerators (a little more complicated for a couple of reasons) were also a fait accompli, and soon would become a fact of life for Britons and their food chain as an ever increasing portion of their diet came from further and farther (in cultural terms) away, and this was not without attendant anxiety (meat image).

The Adams mammoth was front and center for these debates. As mid-nineteenth-century refrigeration technology underwent improvement and refinement, commentators in the popular press drew on the Adams mammoth as proof of concept. A comprehensive overview of meat preservation techniques published in 1869 suggested that “the practical application” of cold “to the preservation of food may have been suggested by the well-known fact that mammoths in a state of entire preservation have been retained for countless ages in the frozen soil of the banks of the rivers running into the sea, on [sic] the north of Siberia.”29 In 1855, an article published in Chambers’s Edinburgh Journal recalled the “huge animal…found imbedded in the ice in Siberia.” “So completely had the cold prevented putrefaction” that “dogs ate willingly of the still existing flesh.”30 Functional refrigerating engines had yet to be built in 1855, but the fate of “Adams mammoth” suggested to the author that what “nature effects on a large scale,”—Arctic freezing, that is—“may be reasonably imitated by man on a more limited one.”31 Refrigerators would be like the Arctic, and like the Arctic, they would be able to prevent putrefaction in flesh—to preserve meat.32 The flesh of the Adams mammoth was evidence of this possibility.

An extended rumination on “Preserved Meats” published in 1852 in Fraser’s Magazine for Town and Country opened with a summation of the Adams mammoth and its significance for both natural history and artificial refrigeration. The “enormous elephant” that was “discovered embedded in a translucent block of ice, upwards of two hundred feet thick…was as perfect in its entire fabric as on the day when it was submerged.” Noting that Cuvier “pronounced it…an animal of the antediluvian world[, w]e might fairly presume,” the author held, “this to be the oldest specimen of preserved meat upon record, and Nature was therefore clearly the first discoverer of the process, although she took out no patent, nor made any secret of her method.33

Once refrigerated ships began to ply the waters between Australia, New Zealand and Great Britain, the Adams mammoth was invoked as part of wider rhetorical efforts to allay the suspicions of British consumers and to naturalize artificial refrigeration. The frozen meat of the 1880s was a great leap forward from the loathsome preserved articles of preceding decades, but even still, the notion that a creature dead for longer than several weeks was fit to eat required a certain amount of cognitive as well as gustatory adjustment.34 [NB Great Britain’s temperate climate] Britons had to be convinced that it was palatable, and early popularizers laid stress on the ways in which “[d]ead poultry, and other articles of animal food” were kept “fresh throughout the winter in many rigorous climates.”35 Less exotic cases, such as that “a dead bullock” in Russia “seen standing erect, a frozen statue, only to be dismembered with axes and saws,” or instances of “bear’s hams” being “sent frozen across the American continent,” were invoked as demonstration of the fact that “animal tissues can be kept practically any length of time in a frozen condition.”36 The Adams mammoth made the same point in the extreme: in 1881, that the great beast’s flesh remained in “excellent condition after preservation for who knows how many centuries”37 served to allay suspicions over the potential dangers of consuming flesh that had been had “been dead from six to nine months, or even longer,” were safe to eat.38

But the way in which this narrative took hold—the evolution of “des glacons” into a great translucent block of ice belied the reality of the conditions under which frozen mammoths were found, and this in turn held implications for collectors and paleontologists struggling to make scientific sense of the phenomenon. So, let us turn now to our second case: the Quackenbush mammoth. NB I’m the only one who calls it that.

3. “A Mammoth Hunt in Alaska”

These are the remains collected by LS Quackenbush in the summer of 1908 for the AMNH. He found them in a bluff at Elephant Point in Eschsholtz Bay a year earlier, when the Museum sent him north in pursuit of what promised to be the find of the century: a seemingly credible report of a full frozen mammoth brought to the Director, H.C. Bumpus, in March 1907, by Edmond S. Meany, a University of Washington professor hoping to entice the larger, more powerful and above all better-funded institution to secure the find. Among the materials Meany forwarded to Bumpus was a copy of a letter from J. Startsman to the “director of exploitation” for the Alaska-Pacific-Yukon Exposition (1909), describing how “an old Indian” had discovered “an extraordinary big” mammoth “imbedded in a glacier” sometime towards the close of the 19th century. In “1898 or 1899,” he took Startsman’s partner to see the animal, and Statesman, in turn, went to see it “in winter time in 1904.”

The potential value of such a specimen—in the words of W.D. Matthew, a curator in the Division of Vertebrate Paleontology, “it would be worth whatever it cost, and would be I think the most valuable single addition we could make to our hall for exhibition…purposes—not even excepting the Warren mastodon or the Brontosaurus.”—was enough to entice the AMNH to take action (even though most such reports were “proved…pure fakes,” in the words of Richard Rathbun, Assistant Secretary of the NMNH),39 and on 5 July 1907, L.S. Quackenbush, whom Matthew had recommended for the job, left for Nome, Alaska, by way of Seattle, arriving there a month later.40 Finding that “there persons concerned in the reported mammoth find would make no reasonable agreement as to showing the body and feeling pretty well satisfied that there was nothing in their various stories,” LSQ tried to make lemonade with a trip to Eschscholtz Bay.41 Here he found “portions of a mammoth skeleton together with some skin and hair…embedded…in such a manner as to afford very good reasons for the belief that the entire skeleton, with a large part of the covering, would be found in place.”42 Concerned about the possibility of competition, Quackenbush kept this find close to the vest—concealing it even from his local assistant, Jim Hoffman— and after he returned to New York in early November he, with the support of Madison Grant, then Sec’y to the NY Zoological Society and notorious eugenicist, petitioned Bumpus for a return visit.

On 19 May 1908, Quackenbush set off once again for Alaska in the high hope of excavating a frozen mammoth. Intent on beating any other collectors, he went overland across the Seward Peninsula (by train and then wagon) to reach Keewalik—the last outpost before Elephant Point—before the first boat from Nome. Again with Jim Hoffman’s assistance, Quackenbush commenced excavation “with picks,” and within a few days could tell that the skeleton was, “unfortunately,” incomplete.43 A hip and leg bones, lower jaw with teeth, two tusks, skull fragments, vertebrae, the tail, and “a small quantity of flesh, skin, hair and wool” were found “crowded together in an area about ten by seven feet.” The rest of the mammoth, Quackenbush surmised,” had “no doubt” been embedded in the part of the cliff that had broken away and been carried off with the land slide.44 The two men finished the excavation by hand, with a great deal of “very hard labor,”45 boxed it up, and set off down the Buckland River to explore its opportunities for fossil hunting. This, too, was a disappointment. Although miners had assured Quackenbush that the Buckland was navigable for 200 miles, extremely low waters made the going difficult, and Quackenbush and Hoffman turned back about 80 miles inland. Overall, the expedition met with little success. “While the results of the expedition have not come up to my expectation,” Quackenbush wrote in his final report to Bumpus, “the collection—though small—and data accompanying it have at least some scientific value.”

In some ways, this takes us onto very different terrain than the conditions that reigned in early 19th-century Siberia. Mikhail Adams was a member—albeit a lowly one—of the St Petersburg Academy of Science, a royally-chartered Enlightenment-like body organized for the production of knowledge. The ruling tsars, from Peter the Great on down, considered the vast tracts of interior lands a little known but strategically important part of their continental empire, and to a significant degree, members of the Academy were engaged in surveying that terra incognita for much of the 18th and 19th centuries46, and so despite his lowly status within the St Petersburg Academy (later chastised for not attending meetings) he had the full power of the state behind him when he collected the mammoth, an arduous and no doubt expensive undertaking.

Quackenbush, by contrast, was a kind of contractor—a “fossil hunter” and naturalist whom the AMNH had relied on only, it seems, reluctantly. Though Matthew’s original recommendation was strong—Quackenbush was “a man in whom we could place confidence” and who had experience collecting with the museum (in Wyoming, in 1903) and Matthew wrote that he “hardly [knew] of any other who knows enough about collecting a fossil skeleton and at the same time has the necessary experience in Arctic travel,” the Museum still would have preferred to send its own man—“[Barnum] Brown or Granger if they could be spared,” Matthew suggested before naming Quackenbush.47 Indeed, Quackenbush was almost benched at the eleventh hour, when a friend of Madison Grant offered to ”examine the entire situation without expense” to the Museum.48 When, at an even later hour, Anderson then bailed, writing most hastily that he found it “impossible…to visit Nome this year,”49 Quackenbush left for Alaska in the company of Madison Grant, who was headed to his ranch near Nome (and, not incidentally, who was given a “free hand” to direct Quackenbush otherwise to his formal instructions if Grant thought the situation so dictated) on only two weeks notice.

And unlike the St Petersburg Academy of Science, the AMNH was itself a privately institution funded by largely by philanthropy. Henry Fairfield Osborn, then Curator of Vertebrate Palaeontology (later Director of the Museum) went hat in hand to J.P. Morgan, Jr., and another wealthy financier Percy Pyne (whose brother was an intimate friend of Osborn) to fund the Alaska expeditions. Over the two seasons (1907 and 1908), each chipped in just over $1,600 (present value conservatively estimated in the range of $20,000 today, with the total for both expeditions estimated in the range of $35,000-$40,000).50 The AMNH, moreover, saw itself in competition with the US National Museum (now the NMNH, Smithsonian) who had sent a collector to the same region in 1904 (Maddren) and would do so again in 1908.

And yet, despite these differences, there is good reason for thinking about Quackenbush’s find alongside the Adams mammoth, and that has to do with the question of just how soft tissue from the Pleistocene era can persist across thousands of years. Flesh, hair, skin, and organs are all normally subject to rapid decay, unlike bone, ivory, enamel—the biomaterials that (largely) compose the fossil record. How the “flesh record” comes to be was a vexing question, and is, largely and with only a few exceptions (such as dry cave preservation, certain kinds of silt found in lake beds, and, of course, bogs and Alpine glaciers) also a circumpolar problem. The explanations that naturalists (and later palaeontologists) posed depended a great deal on how frozen ground was understood. We take for granted that one of the defining features of very cold circumpolar places is permafrost—ground that remains perpetually frozen despite seasonal fluctuations in surface temperature. But as Pei-Yi Chu has shown, the term arose only in the 1920s and 1930s in concert with Soviet efforts to industrialize Siberia. Getting a grasp on the region—the same vast interior tract of territory that Adams and others “explored” under the reigns of successive tsars—spurred the neologism, she argues, whose roots are found in the Russian term for eternally frozen ground, and which entered the English language as “permafrost.” In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, though, writers spoke around what seems to the modern reader an obvious lacuna. For Adams, his journey up the Lena took him through “frozen regions” clad in “eternal snow and ice.” Subsequent translations—at least the more restrained of them—relied on the phrase “rock of ice” to describe and explain the mammoth’s preservative environment. Rock and ice, and the relationship between them, are juxtaposed in some times unexpected ways, and so it is perhaps not surprising that, over time and successive translations, the means of preservation was distorted.

Quackenbush lamented this perversion. The “commonly used term ‘ice-cliff,’” which others employed to describe the high embankments in Eschscholtz Bay, was “obviously erroneous.” Calling directly on the Adams mammoth, with a footnote pointing to Adams’s original in the Journal du Nord, Quackenbush emphasized that “fossils have never been found imbedded in pure-ice-masses and the term is particularly misleading since it implies that preservation of flesh and hair is due to the ice.” In fact, for Quackenbush, careful attention to the complicated relationship between rock (and silt, and muck) and ice that composed the hillock in which his mammoth parts were “entombed” was critical. The complex layering of ice and silt, as well as the vertical cracking of the sedimentary record, were clues that pointed to how, precisely, “mammoth mummies” formed. The layers of silt above and below the mammoth were undisturbed, leading Quackenbush to surmise that the mammoth lay in its position of “primary entombment.” Bits of vegetation—spruce twigs and “blackened willow leaves” and “a small, thin sheet of chewed grass cut out of the frozen sandy silt close to the lower jaw,…as brilliantly green as on the day it grew”51 (Quackenbush’s one concession to poetic imagery in any of his writings)—suggested that “a sudden fall of temperature cannot be called upon, as has been frequently done, to account for the extinction of Pleistocene mammals and the preservation of their flesh.”52 And because what soft tissue remained, he found largely positioned below the hip and leg of the mammoth, Quackenbush suggested that it was the cradle of “muck” that enabled Pleistocene flesh to avert decay—to cheat death.

Conclusion

In the end, the AMNH didn’t get its priceless mammoth carcass, nor did the Alaska-Yukon-Pacific Exhibition. (The closest the latter came to a stuffed mammoth was an elephant composed of English walnuts (of all things), and a lone mastodon tusk in an unrelated exhibition). However, not unlike the Adams mammoth, the tattered remains excavated by Quackenbush and Jim Hoffman did enjoy an exhibitionary afterlife. They remained on display among the Ice Age Mammals from at least 1916 until at least 1958. Even after the AMNH acquired “Effie”—a partial baby mammoth excavated by hydraulic miners in the late 1940s—Quackenbush’s mammoth pieces continued to figure as a sideshow to the Warren Mastodon. For decades, then—almost a century, in fact—if anyone wanted to see a stuffed mammoth, they had to make the pilgrimage to St Petersburg, to see the Beresovka, mounted like a full-body death mask in the position in which it perished. Other than a brief temporary exhibit commemorating her discovery, Effie remained behind glass and behind the scenes in a laboratory in the division of Vertebrate Palaeontology until the fossil halls were comprehensively renovated in the 1990s.53

And while the flesh record fascinated practitioners and the public alike—judging by persistent efforts to find them, and sustained press interest in this class of specimen—until quite recently soft tissue “fossils” seemed to have little to say to palaeontologists that teeth and tusks and bones couldn’t say better. That may be changing as the tools specialists employ—particularly ancient-DNA assays and proteomic analysis—begin to evoke more eloquent testimony about the past, and as some would have it, about the future, too, of the planet. The broader message the flesh record sends, though, is certainly evolving as, with climate change and increasing efforts to extract resources from the far north of Siberia, Canada, and Alaska alike, more and more Pleistocene creatures emerge from the (formerly) frozen ground. Although Quackenbush thought himself unlucky, and his expeditions a failure, seen from another angle he was in fact lucky to find as much soft tissue as he did: these were extremely rare specimens until the latter decades of the 20th century and the early decades of the 21st century, when they have become, if not common, at least less singular. As alpine glaciers throw up the bodies of long-missing mountaineers, and receding polar ice caps reveal the remains of long-lost marine expeditions, so too is the circumpolar permafrost giving up its dead: giant wolves and dwarf mammoths; foals and caribou, and Pleistocene-era people as well, like the so-called Siberian “ice maiden.” In an echo of contemporary media reports where “hundred-year” storms now dominate the news cycle on a yearly basis, these hundred-year finds have become bell-wethers of climate change. More than merely fascinating, the flesh record is an augur of our changing planet.
